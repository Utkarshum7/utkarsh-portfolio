## Summary

I built a support agent for airline customer messages (classify the intent, retrieve similar past answers, draft a grounded reply, decide on escalation) and spent most of the effort on measuring it. On 200 human-reviewed examples it reaches <Claim id="dsi.intent" sidenote /> against <Claim id="dsi.baseline" marker={false} />. The number I lead with is worse: **<Claim id="dsi.escalation" />**, meaning it handled <Claim id="dsi.fn" marker={false} /> on its own. This page is about how I got numbers I trust, and what they say.

## The problem

A support agent that answers confidently but hands off too rarely is worse than no agent. So the questions were: *what does the customer want, can we answer from what the company has said before, and does a human need to take over?* Only the first two are about language. The third is a safety decision.

## Data, and keeping the test honest

- **Source:** the public *Customer Support on Twitter* dataset, filtered to one airline account. I rebuilt conversation threads from reply links: <Claim id="dsi.data" />.
- **Split before anything else:** an 80/20 split **by conversation**, not by message, so no conversation contributes to both the retrieval index and the test pool. A message-level split would have let the agent "retrieve" the answer to the question it was being tested on. <Claim id="dsi.split">(Design notes.)</Claim>
- **Taxonomy from the data:** <Claim id="dsi.taxonomy" />, from reading and clustering real messages rather than a guessed list.

## The golden set: AI proposes, I decide

The <Claim id="dsi.golden" /> was sampled from the held-out <Claim id="dsi.split" marker={false}>20%</Claim>, stratified across intents so rare but important intents (like lost baggage) are represented.

Labelling used the same pattern as the rest of my work: **an AI proposed each label and I decided.** <Claim id="dsi.golden.human" sidenote>Of the 200 gold labels, I approved 85 AI proposals, corrected 109, and labelled 6 directly myself.</Claim> No proposal became a gold label without my review, and the high correction rate is itself a signal I wasn't rubber-stamping. The set was locked before the agent was evaluated, and <Claim id="dsi.gates" /> guard its integrity.

## The agent

- **Intent:** TF-IDF features and logistic regression, trained on the development split with labels from a keyword heuristic (weak supervision), because there were no clean labels to train on.
- **Retrieval:** TF-IDF cosine similarity over past customer-message and company-reply pairs from the development split only.
- **Reply:** assembled from the retrieved evidence, with a check that strips promises the system can't keep (refunds, rebookings, account actions). No LLM was used in the evaluated path.
- **Escalation:** deterministic rules: some intents always escalate; any account-specific signal (booking references, claim numbers) escalates regardless of intent.

**Decision: rules for escalation, not a model.** Escalation is the safety-critical output. Rules are inspectable, testable, and fail in ways you can read. The evaluation shows their limit (coverage), which a person can fix directly from the failure list.

## Results against baselines

Measured once on the locked set, not tuned and re-run.

{/* sourced: dsi.results */}
| Metric (n = 200) | Agent | Retrieval-only baseline | Trivial baseline |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Intent accuracy | **56.5%** | 36.0% | 25.5% |
| Intent macro-F1 | **58.3%** | 35.8% | 4.1% |
| Escalation precision | 83.6% | 100% | 87.5% |
| **Escalation recall** | **51.7%** | 12.4% | 23.6% |
| Escalation F1 | **63.9%** | 22.0% | 37.2% |
| Unsupported claims in replies | 0 of 200 | 0 of 200 | 0 of 200 |

<SourceLine id="dsi.results" />

The agent beats both baselines on every metric except escalation precision. The retrieval baseline's perfect precision comes from escalating almost nothing.

## Where it fails

From the actual predictions, the five largest failure modes:

{/* sourced: dsi.failures */}
1. **Escalation rules miss cases:** 44% of the missed escalations happened even when the intent was classified correctly. A rule-coverage gap, not a model problem.
2. **Thanks and compliments get forced into service intents:** recall for general feedback is only 19.6%.
3. **One intent is over-predicted 30×:** "flight status" was predicted far more often than it occurs (2 real cases), a direct result of noisy heuristic training labels.
4. **The most severe intent is under-caught:** lost or damaged baggage has 46.7% recall.
5. **Always-escalate intents over-trigger** on general policy questions (3 of the 9 false escalations).

## What the headline number hides

{/* sourced: dsi.results */}
- The test set is **stratified, not representative of real traffic**, so 56.5% is not a production estimate in either direction.
- Some per-intent numbers rest on tiny counts (one intent has 2 examples) and aren't stable.
- Escalation F1 (63.9%) is a friendlier number than recall (51.7%). Recall is the one that matters, so it leads.
- I reviewed every label, but reviewing AI proposals can still anchor the reviewer. The correction rate reduces that concern; it doesn't eliminate it.
- "0 unsupported claims" is a narrow, mechanical check (no promised actions), not a measure of reply quality.

## What isn't done

- **LLM-as-judge scoring** of reply quality: harness built, **not run** (no API key during the assessment).
- **Human agreement study:** rating sheet prepared, **none of it rated yet**.
- No multi-turn handling; each message is classified alone. Retrieved answers reflect the dataset's period, not current policy.
- It can't check flights, change bookings or issue refunds. All of those are escalated by design, and the recall figure shows escalation still misses too many.

## What I'd do next

1. Fix escalation-rule coverage using the review notes I already collected. That addresses the largest failure mode.
2. Retrain intent classification on the corrected gold-style labels instead of heuristic labels.
3. Run the LLM judge and the human-agreement study, and report them next to the existing numbers.

## Lessons

- Build the evaluation before trusting the system, and decide in advance which number matters.
- A baseline turns a percentage into evidence; without one, an accuracy figure means little.
- Report the weakness first. Anyone evaluating the work will find it anyway, and saying it first shows you understand the system.
